The launch

  1. Reservoir is a new kind of biological infrastructure. Not a treatment. Not a genetic test. It lets you preserve your own immune cells today and keep them stored for the future.
  2. Your biology changes as you age. The cells you have today are the youngest cells you’ll ever have. We don't know what medicine will be able to do with those cells 20 or 30 years from now. But you can't go back and get the biology you had today.
  3. The insane part is the infrastructure. The problem wasn't figuring out how to freeze cells. That already existed. The problem was connecting blood collection, shipping, cell isolation, cryopreservation, and long-term storage into one system that could actually work at scale. Reservoir built that infrastructure.
  4. You can do it in 15 minutes. Walk into a partner clinic, get a simple blood draw, and Reservoir handles everything from there. Your sample goes through the network, your immune cells are isolated and cryopreserved, and they're stored long-term in liquid nitrogen vapor with redundant monitoring.
  5. The future medicine angle is the real story. Cellino is building technology to make personalized cell medicine possible at scale. Reservoir makes sure the biological starting material is still there when that future arrives.
  6. The founders and technology have been building toward this for years. Nabiha Saklayen started Cellino in 2017, coming from a background in physics and laser technology. Cellino developed technology using lasers and AI to work with individual cells and automate personalized cell manufacturing.
  7. The credibility is already there. Cellino has raised over $120M from Khosla Ventures, 8VC, Felicis, Leaps by Bayer and others, alongside a $25M ARPA-H contract to build autonomous manufacturing for personalized cell therapies.
  8. The launch video tells the story through time. Technology goes from primitive to impossible. Computers can create almost anything. AI can do things that seemed impossible decades ago. Then we hit the one thing we can't recreate: the exact biology you have today. That's why Reservoir exists.

Angle 8

The coolest part of Reservoir is actually the boring infrastructure

People will look at Reservoir and think the crazy part is preserving your cells for the future.

The Infrastructure Moat: But the real startup story is much more boring. They had to build the infrastructure to make it actually work. Think ASML. Or AWS. ASML didn't invent semiconductors. AWS didn't invent the internet. They built the infrastructure that made something incredibly complicated usable at scale. Reservoir is doing something similar for biological preservation: connecting blood collection, logistics, cell isolation, cryopreservation, long-term storage, monitoring and retrieval into one system. And the crazy part is that the individual pieces already existed. The infrastructure connecting them didn't.

Why this angle

  1. Infrastructure stories make complicated technology feel insane. ASML is essentially a global supply chain disguised as one machine. Its systems depend on thousands of suppliers and a network of ~5,100 suppliers today.
  2. AWS is the obvious software analogy: nobody cares about the servers, networking and data centers. They care that the internet just works.
  3. Reservoir is doing the same thing with biology. The interesting innovation isn't inventing cryopreservation from scratch. It's turning a fragmented process into a consumer service.
  4. The FDA-regulated infrastructure makes the story even stronger: this isn't just a biotech demo sitting in a lab. It's building an operational system around preservation.
  5. The punchline is: “The science existed. The infrastructure didn't.”

Why this angle

  1. The money makes the realization hit harder. This isn't some tiny niche. The longevity market is already estimated in the tens of billions, and NewLimit alone is valued at $3.1B.

  3. Reservoir introduces the opposite direction: preserve today's biology and let future medicine figure out what becomes possible.
  4. That's the viral realization. Not “Reservoir is better.” It's “wait, why wasn't preservation part of this conversation?”
  5. The strongest version doesn't attack biotech. It makes people question the assumption underneath it.
